# Mentoring

<div class="ssp-banner" markdown>
**Mentoring is one of the fastest ways to grow, whichever side you're on.** A good mentee asks better
questions, and a good mentor learns to explain clearly, listen properly, and let other people succeed.
This page covers both sides.
</div>

!!! info "Related pages"
    [Talking to Juniors](talking-to-juniors.md) covers day-to-day teaching and code-review feedback.
    This page is about **structured, longer-term mentoring relationships**, inside or outside your team.

## Mentor, coach, sponsor: what's the difference?

| Role | What they do | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Mentor** | Shares experience and advice | "When I moved to tech lead, here's what I wish I'd known…" |
| **Coach** | Asks questions so *you* find the answer | "What options do you see? What's stopping you?" |
| **Sponsor** | Uses their influence for you when you're not in the room | "Kavya should lead the new project. She's ready." |
| **Buddy** | Helps a new joiner settle in | "Here's how deploys work; let's grab lunch." |

Good mentors move between all of these as needed.

<div class="ssp-stack">
<div>1. Agree <span>— goals, frequency, how you'll communicate</span></div>
<div>2. Meet regularly <span>— 30–45 minutes, every 2–4 weeks</span></div>
<div>3. Act <span>— the mentee tries things between sessions</span></div>
<div>4. Reflect <span>— what worked, what didn't, what's next</span></div>
<div>5. Review & close <span>— after 3–6 months, celebrate and decide whether to continue</span></div>
</div>

=== ":material-school: Being a mentee"

    ## Finding a mentor

    Look for someone **1–2 steps ahead** of you on the path you want, not necessarily the most senior
    person you know. Look at your company's other teams, ex-colleagues, meetups and communities.

    <div class="ssp-say" markdown>
    "Hi Meena, I really liked your talk on event-driven design. **I'm working towards a senior backend
    role, and I'd value your perspective.** Would you be open to a 30-minute chat once a month for the
    next 3 months? I'll come prepared with specific questions, and I'm happy to work around your
    schedule."
    </div>

    **Why this works:** it's specific about why you chose them, it has a clear goal, the time ask is
    limited, and you've shown you'll make it easy for them.

    !!! tip "Start small"
        Don't open with "Will you be my mentor?" That feels like a big commitment. Ask for **one
        conversation** about a specific topic. If it goes well, ask for another.

    ## Preparing for each session

    ```text
    Mentoring session — [Date]
    Since last time: led my first design review (went OK; lost control of the discussion)
    Question 1: how do you keep a design review on track when two seniors argue?
    Question 2: should I learn Kubernetes deeply or stay focused on backend?
    Want feedback on: my design doc for the slot service (link)
    ```

    ## Questions worth asking a mentor

    - "What do you wish you'd known at my stage?"
    - "What separates good engineers from great ones, in your experience?"
    - "How did you handle [specific situation]?"
    - "What would you do in my position?"
    - "What am I not asking that I should be?"
    - "Can you review this doc / code / talk and be honest?"

    ## Being a good mentee

    - ✅ **Own the relationship:** schedule the sessions, send the agenda, follow up
    - ✅ **Try their advice and report back:** "I tried your suggestion; here's what happened"
    - ✅ **Respect their time:** finish on time; cancel early if you must
    - ✅ **Say thank you specifically:** "Your advice on design reviews really helped me last week."
    - ❌ Expecting them to solve your problems or get you promoted
    - ❌ Only getting in touch when you need something

    ## Ending gracefully

    <div class="ssp-say" markdown>
    "These last six months have been really valuable. **I've led three design reviews and I'm much
    more confident.** Thank you. I'd love to stay in touch, and I'll let you know how the promotion
    cycle goes."
    </div>

=== ":material-account-tie-voice: Being a mentor"

    ## The first session

    <div class="ssp-say" markdown>
    "Let's start with what you want from this. **Where would you like to be in 6 months**, and
    what's getting in the way right now? Also, how do you like to get feedback: direct, or gentler?"
    </div>

    Agree on:

    - [ ] **Goals:** 1–3 specific outcomes (e.g. "lead a design review", "get promoted to SDE-2")
    - [ ] **Rhythm:** how often, how long, video or in person
    - [ ] **Confidentiality:** what's said stays between you
    - [ ] **Honesty:** "I'll be direct with you; tell me if something isn't helping."

    ## Listen more, advise less (the GROW model)

    | Step | Question |
    |---|---|
    | **G**oal | "What do you want to get out of today's conversation?" |
    | **R**eality | "What's happening right now? What have you tried?" |
    | **O**ptions | "What could you do? What else?" |
    | **W**ay forward | "What will you do, and by when?" |

    **Ask before telling.** Give your own advice only after they've thought it through. Then share it
    as a story: "When I faced this, here's what I did, and here's what I'd do differently."

    ## Useful mentor phrases

    | Situation | Say |
    |---|---|
    | They want the answer | "I'll share my view, but first, what's your instinct?" |
    | They're too hard on themselves | "What would you tell a friend in this situation?" |
    | They're avoiding something | "I notice we keep coming back to this. What makes it hard?" |
    | Giving hard feedback | "Can I share something that might be uncomfortable to hear?" |
    | They succeeded | "That's a real step up. What did you do differently this time?" |
    | You don't know | "I haven't faced that one. Let me introduce you to someone who has." |

    ## Go beyond advice

    - **Open doors:** introduce them to people they should know.
    - **Give visibility:** "Would you like to present this at the architecture review?"
    - **Sponsor:** speak their name in rooms they aren't in.
    - **Share your mistakes:** your failures teach more than your successes.

    ## Mentor mistakes

    - ❌ Turning every session into stories about yourself
    - ❌ Creating a mini-me: their path doesn't have to be yours
    - ❌ Solving their problems for them (they learn nothing)
    - ❌ Breaking confidence, even casually
    - ❌ Cancelling repeatedly; it signals they aren't a priority

    !!! tip "You're never too junior to mentor"
        A 2-year engineer can mentor an intern, and a junior can **reverse-mentor** a senior on new
        tools, AI workflows, or what new joiners find confusing.

## Group & peer mentoring

- **Peer circles:** 4–6 people at the same level meet monthly and each brings one challenge.
- **Mentoring office hours:** a senior engineer holds an open hour every week; anyone can drop in.
- **Book / paper club:** read one chapter or article, discuss how it applies to your work.
- **Communities:** meetups, open source, online forums. Mentors don't have to be at your company.

## Practice conversation: First conversation with a mentor

```dialogue
You: Thank you for agreeing to mentor me. I know you're busy.
Mentor: Happy to. What would you like to get out of this?
You: I want to grow into a senior role in the next year. I think my weak area is leading design discussions.
Mentor: What happens in design discussions now?
You: I usually have ideas, but I don't speak until the end, and then the decision is already made.
Mentor: That's common. What if, in your next design review, you share your view in the first ten minutes?
You: That's scary, but I'll try. Can we meet again in two weeks to talk about how it went?
Mentor: Sure. Send me the date of the review.
```
